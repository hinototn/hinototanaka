Re-Drawing Voting Access

Could Richmond make polling places easier to reach by moving them?

At a glance

Year
2025
Tools
QGIS
Data
Richmond precincts, polling stations, residential addresses, roads and water; households without a car (American Community Survey)
Output
Before, after and change maps; a summary table
Skills
Distance analysis, site modelling, before-and-after comparison, choropleth design

Distance is a quiet barrier to voting, especially without a car. I measured how far Richmond homes are from their polling places, then moved the polling places on paper to see what changed. Access improved in 40 precincts and got worse in 15; the average share of homes more than a mile away fell from 22.9% to 21.2%.

How it was made

  1. Why a mile matters
  2. Moving the polling places
  3. Better in 40, worse in 15

Behind the map

01

Why a mile matters

For every precinct, I calculated the share of residential addresses more than one mile from their assigned polling station. A mile is a short drive and a long walk, so I mapped households without a car alongside it: the people for whom distance matters most.

02

Moving the polling places

Then I tested alternative polling locations, using the pole of inaccessibility (the point hardest to reach) as a guide, and recalculated every distance. Keeping the precinct map, colours and classes identical across the two versions made the comparison honest.

03

Better in 40, worse in 15

Moving polling places helped on average, but not everywhere: improving one precinct can push another farther away. This is a model, not a siting recommendation. Real polling places need buildings, accessibility and approval that a distance analysis can’t see.
