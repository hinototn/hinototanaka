Redlined Chicago

I wanted to see how race, rent, redlining, and green-space access overlap in Chicago.

At a glance

Year
2025
Tools
R (tidycensus), QGIS, QuickOSM
Data
Census tract data on race and ethnicity and rent; a historic redlining index; parks from OpenStreetMap
Output
Four tract-level maps and a comparison table
Skills
Census data in R, spatial joins, choropleth design, urban geography models

Higher rents and predominantly White neighbourhoods extend north of downtown. Many Black- and Latino-majority tracts to the south and west have lower rents, higher redlining scores and less access to green space. Distance from downtown alone can’t explain that shape.

How it was made

  1. Data in R, maps in QGIS
  2. Same tracts, side by side
  3. Rings or wedges?

Behind the map

01

Data in R, maps in QGIS

I pulled census data with tidycensus in R, joined it to tracts in QGIS, and brought in parks from OpenStreetMap with QuickOSM. Every layer ended up on the same tracts, which is what makes the maps comparable.

02

Same tracts, side by side

Rather than stack everything onto one map, I gave each variable its own map at the same extent and scale. Read side by side, rent and racial majority trace the same north–south divide as the redlining index.

03

Rings or wedges?

I compared the patterns with two classic models of the city: Burgess’s concentric rings and Hoyt’s sectors. Chicago’s divides don’t form simple rings around downtown. The maps show overlap, not proof that redlining caused today’s patterns, but the overlap is hard to miss.

Credits & sources
